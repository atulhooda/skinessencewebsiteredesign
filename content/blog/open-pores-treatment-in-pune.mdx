Many people hear that pores can open and close like doors. Some try using hot water, ice cubes, or astringent toners to alter pore size. In human anatomy, facial pores do not work this way. Pores are the tiny surface openings of pilosebaceous units. Each unit holds a hair follicle and a sebaceous gland. Because pores have no muscular valves, they cannot open or shut on command.

Temporary cosmetic fixes often dehydrate the surface or mask the skin for a few hours. In contrast, clinical dermatology looks at the underlying causes of pore dilation. ## Cosmetic Blurring vs Clinical Collagen Induction

Many people turn to adhesive pore strips, alcohol toners, or silicone primers when pores look enlarged. These products may offer a quick visual change, but they do not alter the structure of the pore.

Pore strips pull away surface plugs of oil and keratin. However, this physical pulling often irritates the delicate surface. Dermatologists note that scrubbing or irritating the skin causes inflammation, which makes pores look larger ([What can treat large facial pores?](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/face/treat-large-pores)). In addition, makeup primers only fill surface dips with cosmetic polymers. Alcohol-based toners dry the surface briefly. As soon as the skin hydrates again, the pores look the exact same size.

Clinical treatments take a completely different path. Pores have no muscles around them. Instead, the elasticity and firmness of the surrounding dermis determine how wide a pore appears. By stimulating collagen production, clinical procedures strengthen the structural support around each pore. This structural support tightens the skin frame, creating genuine refinement that lasts.

## Energy-Based Modalities: Microneedling and Energy Devices

When enlarged pores occur alongside loose skin, sun damage, or older acne scars, chemical peels alone may not provide enough structural support. In such cases, dermatologists turn to energy devices and microneedling to remodel deeper tissue.

Fractional microneedle radiofrequency is safe and effective for treating enlarged facial pores ([Fractional microneedle radiofrequency treatment for enlarged facial…](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36038248/)). This device delivers controlled thermal energy into the dermis. The heat encourages collagen remodelling, which tightens the skin around stretched pore borders.

A clinical study of 75 patients confirmed clear gains from fractional microneedle radiofrequency ([Fractional microneedle radiofrequency treatment for enlarged facial…](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36038248/)). After a single treatment session, moderate to excellent pore improvement rates were 13.8% for nasal pores, 8.9% for frontal pores, and 11.1% for cheek pores ([Fractional microneedle radiofrequency treatment for enlarged facial…](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36038248/)). Importantly, the improvement rate rose with the increasing number of treatment sessions ([Fractional microneedle radiofrequency treatment for enlarged facial…](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36038248/)).

Device settings play a vital role in these results. In the study, a longer pulse-width setting of 300 ms was positively linked to clinical efficacy after the first session compared to shorter pulse widths of 100 to 200 ms ([Fractional microneedle radiofrequency treatment for enlarged facial…](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36038248/)). A dermatologist selects these settings based on skin thickness, facial area, and patient needs.

Patients should expect minor, short-lived reactions after the procedure.
